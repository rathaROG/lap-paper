Native Rectangular Linear Assignment with Implicit Rejection:
A Jonker–Volgenant Solver without Square Expansion

Ratha Siv, Matei Mancas, Bernard Gosselin
University of Mons, Belgium
(29 October 2026)
Abstract

Tracking and matching applications often solve rectangular assignment with a uniform unmatched penalty. Padded Python LAPJV interfaces embed the N ×× M costs in an (N+M)-square matrix: for 256 rows and 4096 columns, 144.5 MiB of costs for an 8 MiB problem. We engineer and evaluate Rust (JVRUST), hybrid Rust/C++ (JVHYBRID), and C++ (JVCPP) implementations combining established rectangular JV principles, implicit rejection, float64 working arithmetic, and reusable batch workspaces. Four Windows/Linux and Python environments evaluate LAPX 0.13.0 and laprust/lapcpp 1.2.0, each with 204 cases and 8,946 timing samples. JVRUST and JVCPP beat ordinary SciPy in all 71 required non-batch cases per environment. Against capped SciPy, they win 119–123 and 121–124 of 125 finite-limit cases, respectively; remaining losses reach about 1.16 times capped SciPy’s median latency. Four-worker JVRUST reduces sequential-loop latency by 1.82–5.43 times; for 1024 tiny problems, one-worker batching gives 1.89–2.37 times. All 3,925 compared numerical objectives agree exactly across environments. On two cached video clips, assignment-replay speedups of 1.86–3.26 times translate into smaller Centroid-update gains of 1.04–1.15 times and near-equal SORT medians, without establishing detector-plus-tracker acceleration or a general tracking-accuracy benefit. A separate 67-case LSAPE study distinguishes formulation, specialization, layout, and rejection tracking within Hungarian code. The contribution is software integration and performance evaluation, without algorithmic priority or causal attribution to individual components. Initialization policy selection used development workload families; evaluation is not held out from heuristic development.

Keywords: linear assignment; Jonker–Volgenant; rectangular matching; implicit rejection; rejection penalties; research software; Rust; C++; Python

1 Introduction

Every frame, a multi-object tracker must decide which of its tracks continues which new detection. This is a linear assignment problem with two complications that the textbook formulation leaves out: the two sets differ in size, and a pair whose cost exceeds a gating threshold should not be matched at all. An unmatched track may be occluded; an unmatched detection may be a new object. ByteTrack [1] and trackers built on its association code express this with a single call to a Jonker–Volgenant solver from the lap package [2], passing the threshold as a cost limit. The solver then embeds the N ×× M costs in an (N+M)-square matrix whose dummy rows and columns price each rejection. The same pattern arises wherever correspondences may be missing, in data association, resource allocation, and correspondence estimation. The cost limit prices rejection; it does not require maximizing the number of pairs below the threshold.

The expansion is costly. Consider 256 rows and 4096 columns stored in float64. The original matrix occupies 8 MiB. Zero-padding to 4096 square requires a 128 MiB cost matrix. Modeling unmatched penalties with a 4352 square matrix requires 144.5 MiB. These figures describe the cost matrices alone, before outputs and solver workspace, and the search must also traverse the dummy entries. A native rectangular method can instead operate on the real costs and maintain state proportional to the two set sizes.

Avoiding these transformations is not new in principle. Rectangular assignment, outsourcing, and assignment with insertion and deletion costs already admit compact formulations and specialized augmenting-path algorithms [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9], and tracking toolboxes accept non-assignment costs directly [10, 11]. Volgenant’s rectangular JV with row-reduction initialization (1996) [9] and its structure-aware use for outsourcing by Bijsterbosch and Volgenant (2010) [8] are direct predecessors. Our contribution is software integration and performance evaluation: bounded row reductions, uniform rejection within augmenting search, and reusable batch workspaces, exposed through Python interfaces with the threshold objective of lap.lapjv. SciPy’s modified rectangular JV already avoids required-assignment padding [4, 12]; we therefore compare expanded and compact formulations, including exact capped costs on the original dimensions. These comparisons retain measured practical advantages and counterexamples without claiming algorithmic priority or assigning the residual native-versus-SciPy gap to individual components.

This paper describes such a solver. It combines a Jonker–Volgenant square strategy, derived from the scalar src-d/lapjv approach [13], with native rectangular matching and an implicit rejection terminal that represents a uniform unmatched penalty without dummy rows or columns. The solver is implemented three times with identical semantics: entirely in Rust (JVRUST), in Rust with a C++ square-completion kernel (JVHYBRID), and entirely in C++ (JVCPP). JVRUST is distributed as the laprust package [14] and as the lapjvr family of LAPX [15]; JVHYBRID is LAPX’s lapjvh family; JVCPP is the lapcpp package [16]. All three expose the same single-matrix and batch interfaces.

2 Background and related work

2.1 Assignment algorithms and implementation lineage

Jonker and Volgenant’s algorithm combines initialization, reductions, and a specialized shortest augmenting path procedure [3]. Initial stages often construct much of the matching cheaply. Remaining free rows require augmenting paths that can reassign existing pairs. A square matrix therefore does not eliminate the augmentation phase; it only removes the need for a dimensional transformation.

We use completion to mean the augmenting-path phase that finishes a partial matching. It does not refer to padding the cost matrix.

The baselines are established Jonker–Volgenant families, evaluated through their LAPX implementations [15]. JVX (lapjv, lapjvx, lapjvxa) uses the gatagat/lap-derived dense core [2]; this study times lapjvxa, which returns assignment pairs. JVS (lapjvs, lapjvsa) uses the src-d/lapjv-derived implementation [13]. JVC (lapjvc) adapts the classic dense shortest-path implementation in py-lapsolver [17]. These implementations have different initialization and numeric details. Their shared use of augmenting paths does not imply identical kernels.

SciPy’s native rectangular solver is documented as a modified Jonker–Volgenant algorithm without initialization, referencing Crouse’s treatment [4, 12]. Rectangular solving without square padding already exists independently of the proposed solver. Initialized rectangular JV also predates this work: Volgenant’s RJV omits square column reduction and applies augmenting row reduction from zero column potentials [9]. Bijsterbosch and Volgenant refine RJV and specialize it for k-cardinality and outsourcing, including a capped-cost comparison on the original dimensions and a single relevant external option per person [8]. That unexpanded outsourcing formulation originates with Vander Wiel and Sahinidis, whose shortest augmenting path algorithm treats external assignment through a single unrestricted arc [18]. These initialized rectangular and outsourcing searches are direct predecessors of the state and rejection handling studied here. The contribution concerns software integration and performance evaluation, without priority for these individual principles.

2.2 Optional assignment and compact representations

Optional assignment also has clear precedents. Bougleux and Brun formulate linear sum assignment with edition using a null element on each side rather than a full square expansion [5]. Their augmenting paths can terminate at a null element, representing removal or insertion. The associated LSAPE toolbox supplies C++ implementations, including null-terminal path routines [19]. The 2016 publication is the conceptual comparison; the July-2017 LSAPE-v4 release is the implementation used in our separate controlled experiment. Equality with the author-hosted v4 archive was checked on 27 October 2026, but does not establish identity with the code behind the 2016 timings.

Jones, Chawdhary, and King adapt Volgenant–Jonker methods to graph-edit matching [6]. Their compact representation avoids explicitly storing the redundant zero and infinite regions of the expanded matrix, using only (n+1)(m+1)-1 entries. Bougleux and colleagues subsequently describe a compact reduction from assignment with error correction to a rectangular assignment problem without cost constraints of the earlier reductions [7].

Bougleux and Brun [5] address assignment with element-specific insertion and removal costs through a compact null-element representation and an adapted Hungarian algorithm. The present solver specializes the penalty structure: a common cost L/2 / L 2 per unmatched endpoint yields a one-sided objective with rejection cost LL per unmatched smaller-side row, up to an additive constant (Section 3.3). We realize this specialization within a Jonker–Volgenant implementation combining rectangular reductions, rejection state stored as one distance and one predecessor row, the threshold arguments of lap.lapjv, and native batching. Null-terminal augmenting paths already occur in [5]; we do not claim a new optional-assignment objective, a new null-terminal principle, or an asymptotic improvement over that approach. Table 2 compares the documented designs. Section 6.11 measures the one-sided adaptation within LSAPE-v4; it does not compare our JV implementation against LSAPE or reproduce the 2016 experiments. The native JV code descends from the JVS lineage; no algorithmic priority is claimed.

Application interfaces also accept non-assignment costs. MATLAB’s assignjv, assignmunkres, and assignauction take a scalar cost of non-assignment charged per unmatched endpoint [11]. The Tracker Component Library includes a Jonker–Volgenant routine for missed detections that never builds the augmented matrix but keeps search state for one virtual row per target [10]. The implementation combines a constant-size rejection terminal with rectangular reductions, the threshold arguments of lap.lapjv, and native batch execution.

The compact reduction in [7] is also an executable comparison point. For uniform endpoint penalties, its cost factorization reduces, up to a constant shift, to capping real costs at the combined rejection price. Section 3.5 gives a self-contained argument for this specialization, including finite signed costs, and Section 6.5 evaluates it with SciPy. This provides a baseline without square expansion; it does not reproduce every optimization in the authors’ LSAPE software.

Proof. For any required matching B, let A be its retained subset. Every discarded pair costs L in the capped problem, so the capped cost of B is exactly P(A). 4 Implementation

4.1 Shared architecture and three implementations

Each implementation consists of a thin Python extension and a native core. The extension validates shape, real numeric type, precision, and limit options, prepares a contiguous working representation where necessary, and invokes the core. The single-matrix and batch interfaces, return contracts, and error behavior are identical across the three implementations. Table 3 lists them.

Interface compatibility here means that the single-matrix entry points accept the extend_cost, cost_limit, and return_cost arguments of lap.lapjv with the same objective: the finite limit prices unmatched endpoints, and the reported total excludes those penalties. They return matched row and column index arrays, or pairs, rather than lap’s full-length per-row and per-column assignment vectors, so callers of lap need a small output adaptation. Compatibility does not imply identical assignments when optima tie, or identical behavior across precision modes. At Ci⁢j=L = C ⁢ i j L , matching and rejection tie in exact arithmetic. Native reductions and search prefer rejection on computed ties, whereas the evaluated padded lap.lapjv can retain the edge. This is not a strict Ci⁢j<L < C ⁢ i j L output guarantee: the rounded effective penalty pp in Section 4.5 can differ from the supplied LL.

The native dispatcher selects the square kernel only for square inputs with an infinite limit. The square kernel’s initialization and reductions are followed by an augmentation phase that completes the matching. JVRUST executes this phase in Rust. JVHYBRID calls a C++ completion kernel that operates on Rust-owned workspace and preserves the completion strategy and operation order. JVCPP implements the whole algorithm in C++, including the same C++ completion routine. This design permits two comparisons: of completion kernels within otherwise identical Rust code (JVRUST against JVHYBRID), and of complete Rust and C++ implementations of one algorithm (JVRUST against JVCPP).

All rectangular inputs and finite-limit inputs, including square matrices with a finite limit, use the rectangular core. JVRUST and JVHYBRID share that Rust core, so differences between them on these paths cannot be interpreted as evidence of different rectangular algorithms. JVCPP executes the same rectangular algorithm in C++.

4.2 Native rectangular reductions and search

The rectangular core stores separate mappings, potentials, predecessors, distances, and free-row lists. Following Volgenant’s RJV initialization [9] and its later refinements [8], it omits square column reduction and starts column potentials at zero. Its bounded augmenting row reductions lower a column potential only while assigning that column, maintaining non-positive potentials and zero potentials on unused columns.

After reductions, each remaining free root is introduced with a feasible row potential obtained from its available reduced costs and, in finite-limit mode, the rejection penalty. The root’s reduced costs are retained as the initial shortest-path distances, so the first relaxation does not read the cost row again. A shortest-path search then scans real columns. Reaching a free column completes an augmenting path. Reaching an occupied column continues through its assigned row, allowing previous pairs to be rearranged.

The revised releases use two bounded rectangular row-reduction passes with B=min⁡(c,4) = B min ( c , 4 ) . Each pass permits at most B times its initial free-row count in row scans, including displacement retries. Among equally cheap real columns, a free column is preferred [8]; in finite-limit mode the implicit rejection option still wins its computed ties. For required rectangular matching, max⁡(r,8) max ( r , 8 ) consecutive displacements without acquiring a free column disable further price lowering. Acquiring a free column resets the streak until the cutoff is reached; once disabled, the cutoff persists across both passes. The current scan completes normally. Finite-limit inputs, including optional squares, use the same scan allowance and tie preference without that stall cutoff. Remaining free rows enter augmenting search. These rules limit initialization work without removing admissible edges or changing the objective. Required square problems retain their separate square initializer, which samples four complete rows to select an existing initialization strategy.

The common scan allowance replaces the preceding release’s shape-specific reduction limits. Policy selection used development workload families, including congested and tied costs, and is not a held-out validation of an optimal heuristic. The required-only cutoff limits unproductive displacement chains while allowing optional chains to continue within the scan allowance. Free-column tie scans can add work on heavily tied costs. The complete-call experiments do not isolate the causal contributions of the allowance, tie preference, and cutoff.

4.4 Storage, batching, and operation bounds

For contiguous wide inputs in the selected cost dtype, the real cost matrix can be borrowed. Solver state is 𝒪⁢(r+c) ⁢ O ( + r c ) , with float64 numerical workspace even when costs are float32; widening this linear workspace does not require a dense float64 cost copy. Pair output is 𝒪⁢(r) ⁢ O ( r ) . Tall inputs may require an 𝒪⁢(r⁢c) ⁢ O ( ⁢ r c ) orientation copy, and dtype or layout conversion can also require a dense copy. Including these costs, storage remains 𝒪⁢(r⁢c+r+c) ⁢ O ( + ⁢ r c r c ) , rather than requiring a c-square or (r+c)-square cost matrix.

Batch input storage is shared with the call. Each active worker retains its own workspace and can reuse it across problems; results are collected in input order, and the lowest failing index is reported on error. JVRUST and JVHYBRID schedule problems on reusable Rayon thread pools. JVCPP uses a reusable C++ thread pool and claims consecutive small problems in chunks, bounded to about 4096 costs per claim so that adjacent difficult problems are not serialized on one worker. Wide inputs require no per-worker padded cost matrix; tall problems can require orientation buffers. This distinction is relevant to peak memory as worker counts increase.

Each bounded row-reduction pass performs 𝒪⁢(r⁢B) ⁢ O ( ⁢ r B ) scans of at most c real costs; the additional free-column tie scan has the same order. Since B≤4 ≤ B 4 , the two passes cost 𝒪⁢(r⁢c) ⁢ O ( ⁢ r c ) . Each final augmenting search visits at most r occupied real columns before reaching a terminal, scanning up to c candidates at each step. Across r roots, augmentation costs 𝒪⁢(r2⁢c) ⁢ O ( ⁢ r 2 c ) . Thus 𝒪⁢(r⁢c+r2⁢c) ⁢ O ( + ⁢ r c ⁢ r 2 c ) is a conservative bound for this release’s rectangular solve, excluding separately described conversion and output work. The required-only cutoff can reduce initialization work but is unnecessary for this bound. This is an implementation bound, not an asymptotic improvement over established rectangular assignment methods.

4.5 Numeric and interface contract

All three native implementations retain selected float32/float64 cost storage while using float64 potentials, distances, minima, and reduced-cost arithmetic on required square, rectangular, and finite-limit paths. This extends the earlier square-path arithmetic to rectangular and finite-limit solving. LAPX’s JVS implementation also widens its numerical working state. The correction reduces cancellation when large costs coexist with small assignment differences; it cannot restore distinctions lost during input conversion or guarantee exact optimization for arbitrary dynamic ranges. Finite float64-to-float32 conversion overflow is checked. Other accepted real numeric dtypes first undergo NumPy conversion to float64 without an equivalent explicit overflow check; on platforms with wider real types, a finite value outside binary64 range can become positive infinity at that stage. A motivating upstream report [20] gives the exactly representable float32 matrix (2242241/81/4) ( 2 24 2 24 / 1 8 / 1 4 ) : choosing the diagonal costs 224+1/4 + 2 24 / 1 4 , while the off-diagonal costs 224+1/8 + 2 24 / 1 8 . Accumulating selected original costs in float64 exposes the difference that float32 arithmetic can conceal. This witness motivates regression coverage without establishing robustness for every numerical scale.

For a finite limit, the boundary rounds the half-limit in the selected cost dtype T before promoting it for working arithmetic: h=roundT⁡(L/2) = h round T ( / L 2 ) , p=float64⁢(h)+float64⁢(h) = p + ⁢ float64 ( h ) ⁢ float64 ( h ) . The effective row penalty p need not equal the supplied L. The theoretical equivalence therefore applies to the represented costs and effective penalty. The controlled performance study uses float64 inputs; the application study retains float32 tracker costs and separately widens the same values for replay (Appendix C). The small enumeration study uses binary-rational costs and exactly representable limits, while targeted regressions additionally exercise cancellation, rounded penalties, conversion, and original-input totals. Native output does not apply a raw Ci⁢j<L < C ⁢ i j L post-filter, so computed-tie preference does not guarantee rejection whenever the raw cost equals the supplied limit. Public totals sum selected original input costs and exclude rejection penalties.

Positive infinity represents a forbidden edge. NaN and negative infinity in the matrix are outside the documented supported contract. Required matching can be infeasible, and reduced-cost overflow can also cause an error; these conditions should not be conflated. As defensive checks, a path reversal that does not reach its root and a final matching whose row and column mappings disagree are reported as internal errors rather than returned.

All methods in the controlled assignment timing suites return int64 pair arrays and omit total-cost calculation. Pair ordering remains native to each interface. Untimed validation checks feasibility and objective values, rather than demanding identical arrays when optimal assignments tie.

7 Discussion

7.1 Contribution relative to existing methods

The current native implementations beat ordinary SciPy in all 71 required cases per environment, and Rust wins 119–123 of 125 capped finite-limit comparisons across the four environments, with a maximum latency ratio about 1.16. C++ wins 121–124 of those capped comparisons. These counts refer to specified adapters and measured releases; they do not imply superiority over every compact optional-assignment solver.

The contribution is software integration and performance evaluation within established assignment methods. Rectangular JV with row-reduction initialization from Volgenant (1996) [9] and its refinement and structure-aware use for outsourcing by Bijsterbosch and Volgenant (2010) [8] precede the present implementation. We integrate explicit reduction budgets, uniform rejection inside augmenting search, and reusable batch workspaces in Python-facing Rust, hybrid, and C++ implementations. The full-call experiments characterize that integration without isolating gains from the terminal, reductions, scanning policies, workspace reuse, or output adapters individually.

The refreshed LSAPE study supplies a complementary decomposition within Hungarian code: one-sided costs alone have 20 gains and 41 regressions, while matched-layout specialization exceeds the declared 3% threshold in all 67 cases. The padded total has 38 gains and 18 regressions; the compact total has 35 and 24. Compact layout has no gain beyond the band, and all rejection-tracking estimates remain inside it. These within-LSAPE findings do not explain JV-specific gains. Isolating the JV reduction and terminal policies still requires ablations against otherwise identical implementations. Capped SciPy controls dimensional expansion but differs in initialization, search, temporary storage, and adapter work.

7.2 Performance is determined by formulation and structure

The wide and finite-limit results demonstrate that transformed problem size can dominate practical interface performance. SciPy’s required rectangular results and the remaining finite-limit congestion losses also show why avoiding padding cannot explain every speed difference. The native implementations beat SciPy on the main required block case, yet capped SciPy wins the primary Windows finite-limit block formulation case. Both native methods win that formulation case in the Linux runs. Objective, initialization, reduction policy, cost structure, and adapter work all matter.

The revised constant reduction allowance gives the implementation bound in Section 4.4. Favorable timings still do not establish an asymptotic advance over established rectangular methods. Congestion and ties can change initialization and augmenting-search work, motivating phase-level profiling and matched policy ablations before attributing gains to an individual rule.

The scaling curves retain multiple native-versus-capped ordering changes. The revised policy applies a common scan allowance across shapes and a required-only stall cutoff, replacing the older shape-specific caps. Its development used structured workload families, so these measurements characterize those families without independently validating optimal heuristic choices or predicting arbitrary matrices.

7.3 Implementation language and completion kernel

The three implementations compare implementation choices under a shared algorithmic design. JVHYBRID changes only square completion and provides no consistent whole-call benefit on the evaluated required square workloads. Differences on the shared rectangular Rust path do not establish algorithmic differences; timing variation, adapter overhead, and build effects are not isolated.

Rust is faster than C++ in nine of twelve primary single-matrix cases, while the large-grid and batch orderings vary by workload and environment. Extreme tall inputs retain larger differences. The current Linux runs also change some rankings, including the four-worker batch comparison in Appendix B. None of these comparisons isolates an implementation-language effect.

7.4 Implications for applications

Applications should distinguish required matching from finite-limit matching and benchmark their own shapes, thresholds, and cost structures. The native methods win the required congestion grid, but capped SciPy retains a few finite-limit advantages. Capped costs are not universally the fastest evaluated compact adapter: dummy columns can be faster on extreme tall inputs. The tall-memory difference reflects the extra original-size buffer described in Section 6.9. Native batching benefits independent problems available together; a tracker with one assignment at a time cannot exploit inter-problem batching without changing its work schedule.

An assignment acceleration is not an end-to-end application acceleration of the same factor. Cost construction, motion prediction, feature extraction, detection, and bookkeeping can dominate a tracking pipeline. The follow-up in Section 6.12 measures tracker-update time, supplied-box accuracy and identity switches on two real-video clips, while retaining the distinction from detector-plus-tracker frame rate. Its assignment gains are attenuated in tracker updates, especially for SORT.

Finite-limit integration also requires agreement on ties and numeric precision. Matching decisions at or near a penalty boundary may differ across implementations while retaining an equal or nearly equal objective. Tests should distinguish objective equivalence from a requirement for identical selected pairs.

8 Limitations and threats to validity

The controlled study covers four Windows/Linux and Python environments, four generated inputs per timing case, and nine timing rounds. Dependencies, interpreter builds, binaries, batch placement, and sessions differ. Windows batching uses four logical processors on two physical cores. Linux selects four logical CPUs, but the fresh run records do not establish their physical-core mapping, CPU model, governor, or fixed clock. These data do not isolate operating-system, compiler, interpreter, or physical-core scaling effects. Frequency and background activity are uncontrolled. Timing IQRs describe rounds on the selected inputs, not uncertainty over all assignment problems. Complete compiler flags and source-to-wheel lineage are not independently established.

The controlled runtime tables use float64 costs; the application study additionally uses represented float32 costs and widened views. Targeted regressions exercise the arithmetic correction, including cancellation, penalty rounding, and original-input totals. Float64 scratch cannot recover information lost during conversion and does not ensure exactness over arbitrary dynamic ranges, subnormal penalties, or near-ties. Precision modes beyond the measured inputs are not comprehensively characterized by these runtime tables.

Saved regression records from the archived 28 October 2026 development payload provide a concrete limitation. For float32-stored C=(H2⁢sHHsH) = C ( H ⁢ 2 s H H s H ) , with H=248 = H 2 48 and s=2−12 = s 2 - 12 , the saved development Rust/hybrid and SciPy outputs have required-assignment cost H+2⁢s + H ⁢ 2 s , while the exact optimum is H+s + H s . The exact gap is 2−12 2 - 12 , although both totals round to HH in binary64. Thus equality of recorded binary64 totals is not an exact-arithmetic optimality certificate. This development witness was not rerun against the installed wheels used in the performance campaigns.

The main finite-limit baselines use explicit square augmentation where no equivalent public limit API exists. Section 6.5 adds smaller dummy-column and original-dimension capped formulations, including a uniform-penalty specialization of [7]. The completed LSAPE-v4 comparison in Section 6.11 evaluates formulation and implementation changes within that Hungarian solver. Direct JV-versus-LSAPE comparisons, the exact implementation used for the 2016 timings, and other specialized methods in [6, 7] remain outside the measured scope. SciPy through our adapter is not a substitute for evaluating every specialized LSAPE kernel, and adapter work is part of the reported interface times.

The refreshed LSAPE sessions occurred on the same day/window and host. Three fresh process IDs do not establish independent-day replication. Their observed ranges and 3% band classifications are descriptive; they do not prove significance, equivalence, or robustness across days. The unchanged patch already uses double arithmetic and a guarded rejection-cache fallback, but source review and 10,190 strict cases do not prove arbitrary-range numerical exactness. Neither a general rejection-tracking benefit, LSAPE memory savings, nor isolated preprocessing, code-generation, or kernel-phase effects are established.

The initialization policy was selected using development workload families. A common four-scan allowance and required-only stall cutoff replace the earlier shape-specific reduction limits, but this revision does not establish held-out optimality or isolate the performance contribution of each rule. Fresh seeds alone would not remove family-level selection dependence. Previously saved development validations and the final campaign must not be described as independent policy-selection holdouts.

The controlled assignment suites are synthetic; the application follow-up covers only two supplied 999-frame clips, two detection sources and two simple trackers. Several main finite-limit cases reject no smaller-side rows at the optimum; the formulation experiment covers rejection-heavy inputs and a congested finite-limit case. The scaling experiment covers fixed aspect ratios and a fixed smaller dimension, but uses only four random matrices per size and no held-out application distribution. Its linear and log-log curves are descriptive measurements, not empirical proofs of asymptotic bounds. These deliberately selected cases broaden coverage but do not establish application representativeness. Broader application traces, held-out structured instances and additional detector/tracker configurations would improve external validity.

The base memory observations use one trial per condition; the added sweep uses five fresh processes and paired inputs per condition. Windows peak-working-set and Linux high-water-RSS increments use different operating-system counters and are not pooled. A zero increment can reflect headroom below an earlier process peak rather than zero workspace allocation; such zeros are frequent in the Linux runs. These observations do not establish exact allocation counts, cross-platform resident-memory bounds, or long-running pool-retention behavior.

The three mathematical propositions justify the reformulations and final certificate in exact arithmetic. They do not prove all code paths, error cases, and floating-point transitions. Passing tests and exhaustive small cases complement, rather than replace, that distinction.

9 Conclusion

The paper integrates and evaluates Rust, hybrid Rust/C++, and C++ rectangular JV implementations with bounded row reductions, uniform rejection during augmenting search, and batched Python calls under equivalent objectives. Established initialized rectangular JV and structured outsourcing [9, 8], compact error-correcting methods, and SciPy’s modified rectangular JV provide the precedents. The derivations and implementation bound imply neither algorithmic priority nor a general floating-point correctness proof.

Across four environments, Rust wins 71/71 ordinary required, 119–123/125 capped finite-limit, and five expanded finite-limit comparisons. C++ wins 71/71, 121–124/125, and five, respectively. Remaining native/capped losses reach about 1.16 times latency. Rust four-worker loop/batch ratios are 2.51–3.86 for 64 required problems, 1.82–3.22 for 16 finite-limit problems, and 4.74–5.43 for 1024 tiny problems; tiny one-worker batching gives 1.89–2.37 times. The Linux Python 3.11.18 finite-limit one-worker batch is slightly slower than its loop. These complete-call results do not isolate workspace reuse or individual reduction rules.

All 3,925 compared numerical objectives agree exactly across the four environments, with no recorded timing-cardinality differences. Windows and Linux memory counters require separate interpretation. The independent LSAPE study retains its within-Hungarian formulation and specialization findings, including regressions and no consistent rejection-tracking benefit. On the two supplied video clips, assignment-replay gains became smaller Centroid-update gains and near-equal SORT medians, without establishing detector-plus-tracker acceleration or general tracking-accuracy benefits. Further work should isolate initialization policies, extend independent-day, hardware, and compiler coverage, and broaden application evidence.

B.6 Memory context

The Linux measurements use getrusage(RUSAGE_SELF).ru_maxrss, returned in KiB and converted to bytes, rather than the Windows peak-working-set counter. Each observation is the nonnegative increase of that process high-water mark after the input is prepared and resident. The two operating-system metrics remain separate. They describe observed resident-memory peaks, not allocations or an exact native-workspace size.

Zero increments occur in 309 of 415 Linux Python 3.14.10 observations and 317 of 415 Linux Python 3.11.18 observations, compared with 41 and 36 in the corresponding current Windows campaigns. Linux process peaks before solving can exceed current RSS by as much as 10.00 and 12.38 MiB, respectively. New resident growth below that earlier peak leaves the high-water counter unchanged. A zero plotted increment therefore does not establish zero allocation, and ratios to zero increments are not meaningful.

The Linux derivatives use explicit high-water-RSS terminology, while Windows observations retain the peak-working-set label. Formulation buffer sizes are exact calculations independent of these counters: 8 MiB for the real 256×4096 × 256 4096 float64 costs, 128 MiB for a 4096-square padded matrix, and 144.5 MiB for the 4352-square rejection expansion.

GT detections separate association from detection error; they do not establish real-detector accuracy. Under RF detections, all three proposed native implementations have identical Centroid scores: IDF1 matches SciPy for MOT16-04 and is slightly lower for MOT20-03, while MOTA is slightly lower on both.
