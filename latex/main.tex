\begin{abstract}
Rectangular assignment with a uniform unmatched penalty is often implemented by expanding the cost matrix. We integrate established rectangular Jonker--Volgenant principles, implicit rejection, float64 working arithmetic and reusable workspaces in Rust (JVRUST), hybrid Rust/C++ (JVHYBRID) and C++ (JVCPP) implementations. A single campaign evaluates laprust/lapcpp 1.2.0 and LAPX 0.13.0 in four Windows/Linux and Python environments. Each environment contains 302 cases, 3,286 complete timing conditions and 91 memory conditions, using five inputs and three timing rounds. Against the matching compact SciPy reference, all three native implementations exceed a 3\% advantage on all 102 required cases; JVRUST and JVCPP do so on 163--165 and 163--166 of 166 optional cases, respectively, with ties and losses retained. Geometry sweeps, representation controls, policy ablations, work counters, sparse solvers and orientation pairs qualify these results by workload and interface. Assignment-replay gains are attenuated in tracker updates, and equal replay objectives do not imply identical tracking outputs. A separate 67-case Windows LSAPE study distinguishes formulation, specialization, layout and rejection tracking within Hungarian code. The contribution is software integration and reproducible performance characterization, without algorithmic priority, significance claims or a universal solver ranking. The initialization policy was developed using related workload families; these measurements are not a held-out test of heuristic optimality.
\end{abstract}
\noindent{\small \textbf{Keywords:} linear assignment; Jonker--Volgenant; rectangular matching; implicit rejection; rejection penalties; research software; Rust; C++; Python}

\section{Introduction}

Multi-object trackers associate tracks with detections whose number can differ, while allowing an object on either side to remain unmatched. ByteTrack \cite{zhang2022bytetrack} and related association code express this through the \texttt{cost\_limit} argument of \texttt{lap.lapjv} \cite{gatagatlap}. Its padded implementation embeds the $N\times M$ costs in an $(N+M)$-square matrix. The limit prices rejection; it does not require maximizing the number of below-threshold pairs.

For float64 costs with 256 rows and 4096 columns, the original matrix occupies 8 MiB, square zero-padding requires 128 MiB, and optional-assignment expansion requires 144.5 MiB. These are cost-buffer dimensions, before workspace and output. Compact representations avoid this expansion, but their performance still depends on initialization, cost structure, orientation and interface work.

Rectangular JV, outsourcing and assignment with insertion and deletion costs already provide compact formulations and specialized searches \cite{jonker1987,volgenant1996,bijsterbosch2010,vanderwiel1997,crouse2016,bougleux2016,jones2017,bougleux2020}. Our contribution is to integrate these principles into Python-facing Rust, hybrid Rust/C++ and C++ implementations, then evaluate their complete calls against equivalent dense and sparse formulations. SciPy's rectangular solver already avoids required-assignment padding, and exact capped costs provide an original-dimension optional baseline. Neither dimensional savings nor implementation language alone explains the measured differences.

The paper contributes a self-contained derivation and optimality certificate for uniform-penalty assignment; implementations combining bounded reductions, a reusable rejection terminal and batch workspaces; and an empirical study with explicit representation, policy, work, orientation and application controls. Four environments retain separate measurements and a practical $\pm3\%$ tie band. The study includes unfavorable cases, planned coverage limits and the distinction between assignment latency and tracker-update latency. A separate LSAPE experiment examines related transformations within Hungarian code, without comparing JV and LSAPE runtimes.

Sections 2--4 give the background, formulation and implementation. Section~\ref{sec:design} specifies the saved campaign, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports its results, and the discussion and limitations delimit their interpretation. Appendices organize additional evidence by workload and measurement question. The indexed supplement retains absolute values and provenance for every plotted observation.

\section{Background and related work}

\subsection{Assignment algorithms and implementation lineage}

Jonker and Volgenant's algorithm combines initialization, reductions, and a specialized shortest augmenting path procedure \cite{jonker1987}. Initial stages often construct much of the matching cheaply. Remaining free rows require augmenting paths that can reassign existing pairs. A square matrix therefore does not eliminate the augmentation phase; it only removes the need for a dimensional transformation.

We use completion to mean the augmenting-path phase that finishes a partial matching. It does not refer to padding the cost matrix.

The baselines are established Jonker--Volgenant families, evaluated through their LAPX implementations \cite{lapx2026}. JVX (\texttt{lapjv}, \texttt{lapjvx}, \texttt{lapjvxa}) uses the gatagat/lap-derived dense core \cite{gatagatlap}; the dense campaign times \texttt{lapjvxa}, which returns assignment pairs. JVS (\texttt{lapjvs}, \texttt{lapjvsa}) uses the src-d/lapjv-derived implementation \cite{srcdlapjv}. These implementations have different initialization and numeric details. Their shared use of augmenting paths does not imply identical kernels.

SciPy \cite{virtanen2020scipy} supplies a native rectangular solver documented as a modified Jonker--Volgenant algorithm without initialization, referencing Crouse's treatment \cite{crouse2016,scipylsa}. Rectangular solving without square padding already exists independently of the proposed solver. Initialized rectangular JV also predates this work: Volgenant's RJV omits square column reduction and applies augmenting row reduction from zero column potentials \cite{volgenant1996}. Bijsterbosch and Volgenant refine RJV and specialize it for k-cardinality and outsourcing, including a capped-cost comparison on the original dimensions and a single relevant external option per person \cite{bijsterbosch2010}. That unexpanded outsourcing formulation originates with Vander Wiel and Sahinidis, whose shortest augmenting path algorithm treats external assignment through a single unrestricted arc \cite{vanderwiel1997}. These initialized rectangular and outsourcing searches are direct predecessors of the state and rejection handling studied here. The contribution concerns software integration and performance evaluation, without priority for these individual principles.

\subsection{Optional assignment and compact representations}

Optional assignment also has clear precedents. Bougleux and Brun formulate linear sum assignment with edition using a null element on each side rather than a full square expansion \cite{bougleux2016}. Their augmenting paths can terminate at a null element, representing removal or insertion. The associated LSAPE toolbox supplies C++ implementations, including null-terminal path routines \cite{lsape2017}. The 2016 publication is the conceptual comparison; the July-2017 LSAPE-v4 release is the implementation used in our separate controlled experiment. Equality with the author-hosted v4 archive was checked on 27 October 2026, but does not establish identity with the code behind the 2016 timings.

Jones, Chawdhary, and King adapt Volgenant--Jonker methods to graph-edit matching \cite{jones2017}. Their compact representation avoids explicitly storing the redundant zero and infinite regions of the expanded matrix, using only (n+1)(m+1)-1 entries. Bougleux and colleagues subsequently describe a compact reduction from assignment with error correction to a rectangular assignment problem without cost constraints of the earlier reductions \cite{bougleux2020}.

Bougleux and Brun \cite{bougleux2016} address assignment with element-specific insertion and removal costs through a compact null-element representation and an adapted Hungarian algorithm. The present solver specializes the penalty structure: a common cost \(L/2\) per unmatched endpoint yields a one-sided objective with rejection cost \(L\) per unmatched smaller-side row, up to an additive constant (Section 3.3). We realize this specialization within a Jonker--Volgenant implementation combining rectangular reductions, rejection state stored as one distance and one predecessor row, the threshold arguments of \texttt{lap.lapjv}, and native batching. Null-terminal augmenting paths already occur in \cite{bougleux2016}; we do not claim a new optional-assignment objective, a new null-terminal principle, or an asymptotic improvement over that approach. Table~\ref{tab:methods} defines the measured interfaces and LSAPE arms. Section~\ref{sec:lsape-results} measures the one-sided adaptation within LSAPE-v4; it does not compare our JV implementation against LSAPE or reproduce the 2016 experiments. The native JV code descends from the JVS lineage; no algorithmic priority is claimed.

Application interfaces also accept non-assignment costs. MATLAB's \texttt{assignjv}, \texttt{assignmunkres}, and \texttt{assignauction} take a scalar cost of non-assignment charged per unmatched endpoint \cite{mathworks2018assign}. The Tracker Component Library includes a Jonker--Volgenant routine for missed detections that never builds the augmented matrix but keeps search state for one virtual row per target \cite{crouse2017tcl}. The implementation combines a constant-size rejection terminal with rectangular reductions, the threshold arguments of \texttt{lap.lapjv}, and native batch execution.

The compact reduction in \cite{bougleux2020} is also an executable comparison point. For uniform endpoint penalties, its cost factorization reduces, up to a constant shift, to capping real costs at the combined rejection price. Section 3.5 gives a self-contained argument for this specialization, including finite signed costs, and Section~\ref{sec:representations} evaluates it with SciPy. This provides a baseline without square expansion; it does not reproduce every optimization in the authors' LSAPE software.

\subsection{Interfaces and transformed problems}

JVRUST, JVHYBRID and JVCPP provide native required rectangular assignment and finite-limit rejection. JVX uses square padding or augmentation, and JVS receives a square problem through an adapter. SciPy receives the required, capped, dummy-column, diagonal-dummy or square-expanded representation named in each comparison. The required reference is ordinary rectangular SciPy; the optional reference is capped SciPy throughout the campaign.

The optional sparse comparisons include SciPy's \texttt{min\_weight\_full\_bipartite\_matching} (CSGRAPH) \cite{scipycsgraph} and LAPX's \texttt{lapmod} (LAPMOD). Their measured adapters construct admissible sparse edges and rejection structure from the same dense input. The layout adaptations credit Pylapy \cite{pylapy2026}; the benchmark source retains its MIT notice and identifies the adapted revision. Thus their complete-call times include representation construction. Table~\ref{tab:methods} distinguishes these numerical contracts from the separate LSAPE arms.

\section{Problem formulation}

\subsection{Required rectangular assignment}

Let C be an N \ensuremath{\times} M real cost matrix, with forbidden edges excluded from the admissible edge set E. Orient the matrix so that r = min(N,M) and c = max(N,M), with r \ensuremath{\leq} c. In (1) and (4), \(x_{ij}=0\) for \((i,j)\notin E\). A required rectangular assignment solves

\[
\min_x \sum_{(i,j)\in E} C_{ij}x_{ij},
\quad
\sum_j x_{ij}=1,\quad
\sum_i x_{ij}\leq 1,\quad
x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}.
\tag{1}
\]

The solver must match all r rows if this is feasible. Surplus columns remain unused. If the original matrix is tall, the returned indices are mapped back to the original orientation.

\subsection{Finite-limit assignment}

Let L be a finite limit and A a matching with K pairs. The finite-limit objective is

\[
F(A)=\sum_{(i,j)\in A}C_{ij}
     +\frac{L}{2}(r+c-2K).
\tag{2}
\]

Each unmatched vertex incurs L/2. In the square augmentation used by JVX, the real costs occupy the upper-left block. Real-to-dummy and dummy-to-real blocks cost L/2, while the dummy-to-dummy block is zero.

Equation (2) is not equivalent to solving a required assignment and then discarding expensive pairs. For example, let

\[
C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0.9\\0.9&+\infty\end{pmatrix},\qquad L=1.
\]

Two real pairs cost 1.8. Selecting only the zero-cost pair leaves two unmatched vertices and costs 1.0 under (2). Both nonzero finite edges are below the threshold, yet the smaller matching is preferable.

At equality, a real edge of cost L can tie with rejecting both endpoints. The objective does not impose maximum cardinality among equal-cost optima. Applications that require a particular inclusive-threshold or cardinality rule must specify it separately.

\subsection{One-sided rejection equivalence}

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Proposition 1.} In exact arithmetic, minimizing (2) is equivalent to minimizing

\[
P(A)=\sum_{(i,j)\in A}C_{ij}+L(r-K)
\tag{3}
\]

\end{minipage}
on the oriented real rectangular problem.

\textbf{Proof.} Rearranging the rejection term gives

\[
F(A)=\sum_{(i,j)\in A}C_{ij}+L(r-K)+\frac{L}{2}(c-r).
\]

The final term is independent of A. Consequently, F and P have the same minimizers. The argument holds for any finite real L, including zero and negative values, and includes empty dimensions. \hfill\ensuremath{\blacksquare}

The transformation is useful because every smaller-side row can either select a real column or take a rejection option costing L. No separate dummy column is needed for each possible rejection. The constant must be restored when comparing the full penalized objective, but it does not affect the optimizer.

Signed support is an interface convenience rather than a new uniform-penalty model: adding a constant \(k\) to all admissible real costs and to \(L\) shifts \(P\) by the constant \(kr\) in exact arithmetic. Floating-point execution still requires the numeric contract below.

The public returned total is different again: it sums only selected original input costs and excludes rejection penalties. Our validation and benchmark checks reconstruct the penalized objective rather than confusing it with this reported total.

\subsection{Primal-dual certificate}

For an effective row penalty p, introduce a rejection variable \(z_{i}\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\min_{x,z}\quad&
\sum_{(i,j)\in E}C_{ij}x_{ij}+p\sum_i z_i\\
\text{subject to}\quad&
\sum_j x_{ij}+z_i=1,\qquad
\sum_i x_{ij}\leq1,\qquad x_{ij},z_i\geq0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

This is a network-flow formulation: each row contributes one unit, real columns have unit capacity, and a row may send its unit directly to the terminal at cost p. The integral capacities yield integral optimal solutions. Its dual is

\[
\max_{u,v}\sum_i u_i+\sum_jv_j,
\quad
u_i+v_j\leq C_{ij}\quad((i,j)\in E),\quad u_i\leq p,\quad v_j\leq0.
\tag{5}
\]

\textbf{Proposition 2.} A feasible matching/rejection solution is optimal if a feasible dual satisfies tightness on matched real edges, \(u_{i}=p\) for rejected rows, and \(v_{j}=0\) for unused columns.

\textbf{Proof.} The primal-dual gap can be written as

\[
P-D=
\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(C_{ij}-u_i-v_j)x_{ij}
+\sum_i(p-u_i)z_i
+\sum_jv_j\left(\sum_i x_{ij}-1\right).
\tag{6}
\]

Every term is nonnegative by primal and dual feasibility. The stated conditions make each term zero, so the primal and dual objectives coincide. Weak duality establishes optimality. \hfill\ensuremath{\blacksquare}

For required rectangular assignment, rejection variables and the constraints \(u_{i}\leq p\) are omitted. The non-positive column potentials and zero potentials on unused columns remain important. Ordinary square reductions cannot be transferred blindly to a rectangular implementation.

These propositions establish the formulation and a final optimality certificate. They are not a formal verification of every implementation transition or a floating-point error bound.

\subsection{Compact equivalent formulations}

The one-sided objective also permits two formulations for an ordinary required rectangular solver. First, append r dummy columns of cost L to the oriented real matrix. The resulting r \ensuremath{\times} (c+r) problem assigns every row either to a real column or to one of the distinct dummy columns. Removing dummy pairs recovers an optimal finite-limit matching under (3).

Second, no additional columns are necessary when rejection costs are uniform. Define a capped matrix on the original r \ensuremath{\times} c dimensions:

\[
\widehat C_{ij}=\min(C_{ij},L),
\qquad \min(+\infty,L)=L.
\tag{7}
\]

\textbf{Proposition 3.} Let B be a minimum-cost required matching of all r rows under (7). Retain only its pairs whose original cost is strictly below L. The retained matching minimizes (3); rejecting equality ties is one valid optimality convention.

\textbf{Proof.} For any required matching B, let A be its retained subset. Every discarded pair costs L in the capped problem, so the capped cost of B is exactly P(A). Conversely, any finite-limit matching A can be extended to r pairs because c\ensuremath{\geq}r. Each of the added pairs costs at most L after capping, and each original pair costs at most its original cost. Thus some required matching has capped cost at most P(A). Taking minima in both directions proves equality of the optimal values and optimality of the retained subset. The argument holds for finite real L and finite admissible costs, including negative values; forbidden original pairs are discarded. \hfill\ensuremath{\blacksquare}

This is the uniform-penalty case of the substitution factorization underlying \cite{bougleux2020}, stated here to make the comparison reproducible. Under this specialization, Bougleux et al.\ (Theorem 1, Eqs. (7)--(9)) retain selected substitutions at equality, whereas Proposition 3 and the capped SciPy adapter discard them. Both conventions preserve the optimal penalized objective in exact arithmetic, but may return different pairs and cardinalities. Capping occurs before optimization. It is therefore different from solving the original required problem and then discarding expensive pairs, which fails in the example in Section 3.2. The capped and dummy-column baselines are equivalent in exact arithmetic; their floating-point behavior is assessed separately by the validation experiments.

Table~\ref{tab:methods} gives the representation dimensions and objectives. A reusable terminal is a search option, not a capacity-one dummy column. These dimensions describe buffers, not measured process memory.

\section{Implementation}

\subsection{Shared architecture and three implementations}

Each implementation consists of a thin Python extension and a native core. The extension validates shape, real numeric type, precision, and limit options, prepares a contiguous working representation where necessary, and invokes the core. The single-matrix and batch interfaces, return contracts, and error behavior are identical across the three implementations. JVRUST is provided by laprust 1.2.0 and the \texttt{lapjvr} family in LAPX 0.13.0; JVHYBRID by LAPX \texttt{lapjvh}; and JVCPP by lapcpp 1.2.0 \cite{laprust2026,lapx2026,lapcpp2026}.


Interface compatibility here means that the single-matrix entry points accept the \texttt{extend\_cost}, \texttt{cost\_limit}, and \texttt{return\_cost} arguments of \texttt{lap.lapjv} with the same objective: the finite limit prices unmatched endpoints, and the reported total excludes those penalties. They return matched row and column index arrays, or pairs, rather than lap's full-length per-row and per-column assignment vectors, so callers of lap need a small output adaptation. Compatibility does not imply identical assignments when optima tie, or identical behavior across precision modes. At \(C_{ij}=L\), matching and rejection tie in exact arithmetic. Native reductions and search prefer rejection on computed ties, whereas the evaluated padded \texttt{lap.lapjv} can retain the edge. This is not a strict \(C_{ij}<L\) output guarantee: the rounded effective penalty \(p\) in Section 4.5 can differ from the supplied \(L\).

The native dispatcher selects the square kernel only for square inputs with an infinite limit. The square kernel's initialization and reductions are followed by an augmentation phase that completes the matching. JVRUST executes this phase in Rust. JVHYBRID calls a C++ completion kernel that operates on Rust-owned workspace and preserves the completion strategy and operation order. JVCPP implements the whole algorithm in C++, including the same C++ completion routine. This design permits two comparisons: of completion kernels within otherwise identical Rust code (JVRUST against JVHYBRID), and of complete Rust and C++ implementations of one algorithm (JVRUST against JVCPP).

All rectangular inputs and finite-limit inputs, including square matrices with a finite limit, use the rectangular core. JVRUST and JVHYBRID share that Rust core, so differences between them on these paths cannot be interpreted as evidence of different rectangular algorithms. JVCPP executes the same rectangular algorithm in C++.

\subsection{Native rectangular reductions and search}

The rectangular core stores separate mappings, potentials, predecessors, distances, and free-row lists. Following Volgenant's RJV initialization \cite{volgenant1996} and its later refinements \cite{bijsterbosch2010}, it omits square column reduction and starts column potentials at zero. Its bounded augmenting row reductions lower a column potential only while assigning that column, maintaining non-positive potentials and zero potentials on unused columns.

After reductions, each remaining free root is introduced with a feasible row potential obtained from its available reduced costs and, in finite-limit mode, the rejection penalty. The root's reduced costs are retained as the initial shortest-path distances, so the first relaxation does not read the cost row again. A shortest-path search then scans real columns. Reaching a free column completes an augmenting path. Reaching an occupied column continues through its assigned row, allowing previous pairs to be rearranged.

The implementations use two bounded rectangular row-reduction passes with \(B=\min(c,4)\). Each pass permits at most B times its initial free-row count in row scans, including displacement retries. Among equally cheap real columns, a free column is preferred \cite{bijsterbosch2010}; in finite-limit mode the implicit rejection option still wins its computed ties. For required rectangular matching, \(\max(r,8)\) consecutive displacements without acquiring a free column disable further price lowering. Acquiring a free column resets the streak until the cutoff is reached; once disabled, the cutoff persists across both passes. The current scan completes normally. Finite-limit inputs, including optional squares, use the same scan allowance and tie preference without that stall cutoff. Remaining free rows enter augmenting search. These rules limit initialization work without removing admissible edges or changing the objective. Required square problems retain their separate square initializer, which samples four complete rows to select an existing initialization strategy.

Policy selection used development workload families, including congested and tied costs, and is not a held-out validation of an optimal heuristic. The required-only cutoff limits unproductive displacement chains while allowing optional chains to continue within the scan allowance. Free-column tie scans can add work on heavily tied costs. Separately built policy variants test these choices within matched implementations (Section~\ref{sec:policy}); they do not assign a causal share of the native-versus-SciPy difference to any rule.

\subsection{Implicit rejection terminal}

For finite-limit matching, the search compares real-column paths with a rejection terminal. A real edge has reduced cost \(C_{ij}-u_i-v_j\). Leaving a row unmatched has reduced cost \(p-u_i\). The implementation stores the best terminal distance and its predecessor row using the variables \texttt{dummy\_distance} and \texttt{dummy\_row}.

More precisely, let \(\delta_i\) be the shortest-path distance at which row i is reached; the root has \(\delta=0\). When row i is reached, the rejection candidate \(\delta_i+p-u_i\) updates the terminal distance \(D_\perp\), the smallest such value so far, and its row. Each unsettled real column j is relaxed to \(\min(d_j,\ \delta_i+C_{ij}-u_i-v_j)\). If \(D_\perp\) is no greater than the smallest unsettled column distance, rejection is selected; otherwise that column is settled, and the search either ends at a free column or continues through the column's matched row. The terminal therefore adds one distance and one predecessor row to the ordinary search state.

Let D be the distance of the selected endpoint. The root's potential increases by D. Every settled column j has its potential lowered by \(D-d_j\) and, if matched, its row's potential raised by the same amount. Assignments are then reversed from the free column, or from the terminal's predecessor row, back to the root. A free column ending the path has \(d_j=D\) and keeps its zero potential, so column potentials stay non-positive and unused columns stay at zero.

If rejection is selected, the number of real pairs need not increase: the path can replace one row's real assignment and leave another row unmatched.

Figure~\ref{fig:paths} in the appendix illustrates these two search endpoints.


Algorithm 1 summarizes the distinction between this representation and explicit matrix augmentation.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{High-level native rectangular control flow with implicit rejection. The implementation additionally specifies reduction budgets, finite-value checks, tie handling, and workspace reuse.}
\label{alg:1}
\begin{lstlisting}
Orient C so that r <= c.
Initialize mappings, potentials, and free-row state.
Apply bounded row reductions, including rejection choices in finite-limit mode.
For every row still requiring processing:
    Establish the root row potential from real reduced costs and finite p.
    Search shortest alternating paths over real columns.
    In finite-limit mode, also track the best rejection terminal.
    Stop at a free real column or the selected rejection terminal.

\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Storage, batching, and operation bounds}

For contiguous wide inputs in the selected cost dtype, the real cost matrix can be borrowed. Solver state is \(\mathcal{O}(r+c)\), with float64 numerical workspace even when costs are float32; widening this linear workspace does not require a dense float64 cost copy. Pair output is \(\mathcal{O}(r)\). Tall inputs may require an \(\mathcal{O}(rc)\) orientation copy, and dtype or layout conversion can also require a dense copy. Including these costs, storage remains \(\mathcal{O}(rc+r+c)\), rather than requiring a c-square or (r+c)-square cost matrix.

Batch input storage is shared with the call. Each active worker retains its own workspace and can reuse it across problems; results are collected in input order, and the lowest failing index is reported on error. JVRUST and JVHYBRID schedule problems on reusable Rayon thread pools. JVCPP uses a reusable C++ thread pool and claims consecutive small problems in chunks, bounded to about 4096 costs per claim so that adjacent difficult problems are not serialized on one worker. Wide inputs require no per-worker padded cost matrix; tall problems can require orientation buffers. This distinction is relevant to peak memory as worker counts increase.

Each bounded row-reduction pass performs \(\mathcal{O}(rB)\) scans of at most c real costs; the additional free-column tie scan has the same order. Since \(B\leq4\), the two passes cost \(\mathcal{O}(rc)\). Each final augmenting search visits at most r occupied real columns before reaching a terminal, scanning up to c candidates at each step. Across r roots, augmentation costs \(\mathcal{O}(r^2c)\). Thus \(\mathcal{O}(rc+r^2c)\) is a conservative bound for this release's rectangular solve, excluding separately described conversion and output work. The required-only cutoff can reduce initialization work but is unnecessary for this bound. This is an implementation bound, not an asymptotic improvement over established rectangular assignment methods.

\subsection{Numeric and interface contract}

All three native implementations retain selected float32/float64 cost storage while using float64 potentials, distances, minima, and reduced-cost arithmetic on required square, rectangular, and finite-limit paths. LAPX's JVS implementation also widens its numerical working state. The wider working arithmetic reduces cancellation when large costs coexist with small assignment differences; it cannot restore distinctions lost during input conversion or guarantee exact optimization for arbitrary dynamic ranges. Finite float64-to-float32 conversion overflow is checked. Other accepted real numeric dtypes first undergo NumPy conversion to float64 without an equivalent explicit overflow check; on platforms with wider real types, a finite value outside binary64 range can become positive infinity at that stage. A motivating upstream report \cite{lapjvprecision2026} gives the exactly representable float32 matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2^{24}&2^{24}\\1/8&1/4\end{smallmatrix}\right)\): choosing the diagonal costs \(2^{24}+1/4\), while the off-diagonal costs \(2^{24}+1/8\). Accumulating selected original costs in float64 exposes the difference that float32 arithmetic can conceal. This witness motivates regression coverage without establishing robustness for every numerical scale.

For a finite limit, the boundary rounds the half-limit in the selected cost dtype T before promoting it for working arithmetic: \(h=\operatorname{round}_T(L/2)\), \(p=\mathrm{float64}(h)+\mathrm{float64}(h)\). The effective row penalty p need not equal the supplied L. The theoretical equivalence therefore applies to the represented costs and effective penalty. The controlled performance study uses float64 inputs; the application study retains float32 tracker costs and separately widens the same values for replay (Appendix~\ref{app:applications}). Saved validation checks the represented objective and targeted numerical and interface cases; it does not establish arbitrary-range exactness. Native output does not apply a raw \(C_{ij}<L\) post-filter, so computed-tie preference does not guarantee rejection whenever the raw cost equals the supplied limit. Public totals sum selected original input costs and exclude rejection penalties.

Positive infinity represents a forbidden edge. NaN and negative infinity in the matrix are outside the documented supported contract. Required matching can be infeasible, and reduced-cost overflow can also cause an error; these conditions should not be conflated. As defensive checks, a path reversal that does not reach its root and a final matching whose row and column mappings disagree are reported as internal errors rather than returned.

All methods in the controlled assignment timing suites return int64 pair arrays and omit total-cost calculation. Pair ordering remains native to each interface. Untimed validation checks feasibility and objective values, rather than demanding identical arrays when optimal assignments tie.

\subsection{Workload coverage and estimators}

Each environment has 302 cases: 268 single matrices (102 required and 166 optional), 14 batch/workspace workloads and 20 memory cases. The 3,377 planned jobs comprise 3,286 timing conditions and 91 memory conditions. All are complete, with no malformed records or unobserved planned timing conditions. Geometry contributes 120 single-matrix cases; the remaining families cover base inputs, formulations, size and aspect-ratio scaling, large and extreme shapes, congestion, forbidden entries, ties, signed costs and rejection limits.

Non-geometric synthetic costs start from independent $U_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1)$. Signed costs use $2U-1$. The congested-block family adds 3 to the two off-diagonal blocks formed by the first $3r/4$ rows and $c/4$ columns (integer floors); the congestion sweep varies the row fraction over $0.25,0.5,0.625,0.75,0.9$ at column fraction $0.25$. Quantized costs use $\lfloor kU\rfloor/k$ for $k\in\{1,2,8,256\}$. The forbidden-entry sweep masks independent entries with probabilities $0,0.5,0.9,0.99$ while retaining a feasible diagonal. Its optional limit is $0.00625$, compared with $0.5$ for congestion and $0.25$ for the tie sweep. Individual base and scaling cases retain their manifest-specific parameters.

The frozen generator preserves legacy PCG64 streams and offsets; it does not derive every input from one common seed. Extended families use seed 20261024 with a group-key hash and input index; geometry and supplemental inputs use seed 20261030 with the full SHA-256 group-key digest and input index. The manifests and source snapshots retain the legacy seeds, stream offsets and exact construction rules. Input hashes identify the actual measured corpus.

Every assignment timing condition uses five frozen inputs, three rounds and a 20 ms target block. The estimate is the median of three round means, with equal weight to each input within a round. The reported reference/method ratio divides these estimates from the same successful paired attempt; it is not a median of input-wise ratios. Required cases use ordinary SciPy and optional cases use capped SciPy as their primary comparator. JVHYBRID's canonical coverage is required-only; its absence in optional panels is planned.

Ratios below 0.97 are losses, values in $[0.97,1.03]$ are practical ties, and values above 1.03 are wins. This band is descriptive. We neither pool environments nor interpret timing-round spread as independent-session uncertainty. Derived tall/wide contrasts join two matched-corpus case attempts and are explicitly distinct from the saved same-attempt ratios. All estimates, references, input identities, attempts, status and reasons remain in the indexed supplement.

\subsection{Geometry, representations and controlled policies}

The tracking-like geometry design is inspired by Pylapy \cite{pylapy2026}. It draws $r$ source points uniformly in the unit square, selects $\mathrm{round}(qr)$ survivors without replacement, and perturbs each surviving coordinate by an independent zero-mean Gaussian with standard deviation $m\delta$, with motion multiplier $m\in\{0.25,1\}$. It adds $c-\mathrm{round}(qr)$ new uniform points, shuffles the observations, and uses Euclidean distances as costs. Moved points are not clipped or wrapped back into the square. Tracking-like geometry spans 8--4096 rows with square and $5:4$ shapes. The size sweeps retain two settings at every size: high survival/low motion ($q=0.9$, $m=0.25$) and low survival/high motion ($q=0.6$, $m=1$), where $\delta=r^{-1/2}$ is the generator's characteristic spacing. The complete $256\times320$ and $1024\times1280$ panels retain all four survival/motion settings. The former also varies rejection limit and gate, including the primary cases deduplicated into that grid. Connections in the figures hold the generator, objective, limit policy, orientation and environment fixed apart from the named sweep variable.

In geometry, JVX, JVS and square-expanded SciPy are not planned above 1024 rows. This also stops unpadded required-square JVX/JVS. It is an additional light-profile rule, distinct from the general admission limit on padded buffers of $4096^2$ entries. Missing points beyond a declared cutoff are not timing failures or evidence of a timeout. The native and matching compact SciPy comparisons continue to the largest sizes. Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity} examines the protocol reduction using a separate saved full session, without adding its timings to the primary results.

The formulation and padded-layout panels separate mathematical representation from placement of real and dummy costs. The controlled policy panel uses separately built Rust and C++ variants with matched costs and output contracts. It varies bounded reduction budgets, bypasses reductions, disables free-column tie preference, and disables the required-only stall cutoff where applicable. Primary latency and separately instrumented work are joined by case, policy and input identity. Work-event counts are descriptive operations, not interchangeable units of processor time.

\subsection{Validation, batch controls and memory}

The saved campaign validates feasible outputs and their penalized objectives against the specified reference, checks represented costs and input hashes, and retains rejection cardinalities. Pair-array equality is not required when optimal solutions tie. Diagnostics have their own build and environment identities and are matched to primary input hashes. Unsupported fixtures and these boundary records are separate from the complete primary timing inventory. Primary input validation uses relative/absolute objective tolerances $10^{-11}$/$10^{-12}$. These checks support the sampled cases, not a proof of all floating-point transitions.

Batch contrasts distinguish Python loops, one-worker batch dispatch, four-worker execution, and controlled workspace lifetimes. Ratios are formed within each workload and environment; absolute batch times are retained separately. Repeated workspaces are tested directly rather than inferred from a single-call comparison. Independent problems must already be available together for inter-problem parallelism to apply.

Every memory condition has five fresh-process trials with resident input before baseline and a 50 ms output hold. Peak capture precedes reference validation. \subsection{Application and LSAPE contracts}

The application suite reuses supplied 999-frame excerpts of MOT16-04 and MOT20-03 \cite{milan2016mot16,dendorfer2020mot20}, with supplied ground-truth boxes or cached RF-DETR Nano detections \cite{rfdetr2025software}. The detector cache used RF-DETR 1.10.1 at 768-pixel resolution and confidence 0.2. Centroid and SORT \cite{bewley2016sort} come from pyppbox 4.0.0 \cite{pyppbox2026,siv2022posetreid}, pinned to source commit \texttt{f3f1e8e671d7}. Dataset/cache modifications and third-party notices are recorded in \path{benchmark/application/ATTRIBUTION.md}. Three rounds measure tracker updates and replay of the common captured SciPy matrices. Replay retains the float32 values and an exactly widened float64 view; it measures assignment calls separately from tracker updates. Detector inference, decoding and rendering are outside both boundaries. All eight APIs, including three distinct JVX return interfaces, remain in the supplement. The stock Centroid distance gate and SORT IoU post-filter and unique-feasible bypass are preserved; finite-limit assignment is not substituted into the trackers. The adapter normalizes application output to int32 pairs, unlike the controlled campaign's int64 pairs. Accuracy uses supplied-box motmetrics 1.4.0 \cite{motmetrics2026} evaluation at IoU $\geq0.5$, without the official dataset class/ignore policy. Accuracy and prediction checks are reported separately from objective checks.

The Windows-only LSAPE study uses the July-2017 LSAPE-v4 Hungarian implementation \cite{lsape2017}, with five arms defined in Table~\ref{tab:methods}. It retains 67 cases, four shared inputs, nine seeded-shuffled rounds with a 30 ms calibration target, and three fresh processes per Python environment. This is a frozen protocol rather than the assignment campaign's three-round estimator. Each process estimates a paired-round geometric-mean ratio; the reported central value and range are the median and observed minimum/maximum of the three process estimates. These ranges are descriptive, not confidence intervals.

With $h=\mathrm{float64}(L/2)$ and $p=h+h$, equal endpoint costs $(h,h)$ and one-sided costs $(p,0)$ differ by $h(m-n)$; comparisons restore the same full objective. $O/U$ changes formulation within unchanged upstream code, $U/P$ measures specialization on the same full buffer and stride, $S/T$ changes rejection tracking at fixed compact layout, and $P/T$ changes padded versus compact tracked representation. The specialization also includes indexing and parameter-passing changes, so its components are not individually isolated. All arms copy the real costs; compact storage removes only $n+m+1$ doubles. The frozen bridge accepts finite nonnegative binary64 costs and limits bounded by $\mathrm{DBL\_MAX}/[16(n+m+1)]$, with $(n+1)(m+1)\leq\mathrm{INT\_MAX}$, round-to-nearest and gradual underflow. Signed costs, forbidden edges and required assignment are outside this experiment's contract. Guarded rejection caching falls back to the ordered scan outside its stated numerical range. This experiment introduces no JV row reductions and supplies no JV-versus-LSAPE ranking or measured memory benefit.

\section{Discussion}

The native implementations combine established initialized rectangular JV and uniform-rejection ideas in usable interfaces. Their required and optional results support a practical advantage over the specified compact SciPy references on most measured cases, with congested-block exceptions retained. The capped reference is essential: comparing only with square expansion would conflate avoiding dummy costs with the remaining implementation differences.

The controls make the interpretation more specific. Representation and layout panels separate alternative buffers; policy variants change individual initialization choices within matched builds; counters expose a shift between initializer and completion work; batch controls distinguish dispatch, workers and retention; and orientation pairs reveal different complete-call responses to the same costs. None of these comparisons assigns the entire native/SciPy difference to one component. The sparse comparisons also show why a favorable ratio against one baseline is insufficient to establish a solver ranking.

JVRUST and JVHYBRID share the rectangular Rust core, so differences there cannot establish a different rectangular algorithm. Their required-square comparison changes the completion kernel within the shared design. JVCPP changes more of the implementation and build. The observed differences therefore describe implementations, not an intrinsic Rust-versus-C++ effect. Likewise, matched package versions and verified placement improve environmental control without turning separate operating-system and interpreter sessions into causal experiments.

For applications, the assignment objective, tie convention, represented precision and output interface must match the intended contract. Replay isolates common matrix calls but is not the evolving tracker workload after a different matching decision. Tracker updates include work beyond assignment, and detector inference adds another boundary. The observed attenuation and prediction differences justify benchmarking the actual pipeline rather than extrapolating an isolated solver speedup.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Limitations and threats to validity}

There is one primary session per environment, on one recorded processor model. The study does not estimate variation across days, machines or compilers. Frequency, power-management behavior and background activity remain uncontrolled. Three rounds over five frozen inputs give descriptive within-session estimates, not population confidence intervals or significance tests. Within-attempt rounds cannot detect a persistent attempt-level shift. The seven post-hoc reference discrepancies in Section~6.5 show why reference stability matters, but interpreter, build and collection session change together; the data cannot identify a causal attempt effect. All primary observations and classifications are retained. The practical tie band is a reporting convention, and a tie does not prove numerical or performance equivalence.

The initialization heuristic was selected using related workload families. Controlled ablations characterize the selected panel, but do not establish a held-out optimum or universal policy. The synthetic geometry is tracking-like rather than a sample from the application corpus. The light profile deliberately omits two geometry settings outside the complete panels and stops selected baselines above 1024 rows. No missing observation is extrapolated. The separate full-session sensitivity check supports a bounded reporting choice, not statistical equivalence of protocols.

The proofs hold in exact arithmetic. Float64 working state cannot recover distinctions lost during cost conversion and does not prove arbitrary-range floating-point optimality. Equal recorded floating-point objective totals can conceal smaller exact differences. Pair identities and cardinality can differ among equal-cost optima, especially at the rejection limit. Saved validation supports the actual sampled inputs and represented objectives, not every code transition or error path.

The applications cover two cached clips, two detection sources and two trackers. Detector preparation is input provenance, not a newly measured stage. Replay gains and tracker-update gains cannot establish a general full-pipeline speedup, and mixed tracking metric changes cannot establish an accuracy benefit. The frozen LSAPE contract is narrower than the JV interface and its process replications remain Windows-only. It is not a direct comparison with JV or every compact error-correcting algorithm.

Memory counters have platform-specific semantics and prior-peak headroom. Neither a zero increment nor final resident growth measures total allocation. The trials do not establish cross-platform memory efficiency or long-running pool retention. Constructor timings, instrumented events and primary complete-call timings have different boundaries and must not be interchanged.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}

Native rectangular rejection integrates established assignment principles without requiring square expansion. In each of four environments, the measured Rust, hybrid and C++ implementations win all 102 required comparisons against rectangular SciPy under a 3\% practical band. Optional results remain favorable on most of the 166 cases, with congested-block ties and losses in the formulation, scaling and congestion families against capped SciPy. Representation, policy, work, sparse, batch and orientation controls make these results conditional on the workload and complete interface. Application replay gains attenuate in tracker updates, and the LSAPE controls remain a separate within-Hungarian experiment. The reproducible saved-data figures and indexed evidence support these measured claims without implying algorithmic priority, universal superiority or unmeasured application benefits.
